# PER LA PATRIA MORII IO
### *For My Homeland, I Died*

*Seven nights, seven stories, one grandfather, and no Wi-Fi*

---

## A Note Before We Begin

This is a book of stories that are funny and sad at the same time, because real life is like that.

Every chapter tells the story of a real person who gave their life for their country. Their names have been changed, so you can meet them as ordinary people first: people who got nervous, made jokes, made mistakes, and loved their families.

At the end of the book you will find out who they really were.

At the end of every chapter there is a short lesson from the Christian faith. These people did not die because they loved death. They died because they loved something more than their own lives.

---

## PROLOGUE: The Wi-Fi Is Dead

On the first night of summer, in a small village on the coast near Rome, the Wi-Fi died.

Luca Ferraro, fourteen years old, held his phone up toward the ceiling like a priest lifting the Host at Mass. Nothing. He walked into the garden and held it toward the sky. Nothing. He climbed onto the old stone wall and held it toward the sea.

"If you fall off that wall," said a voice from the porch, "I'm not carrying you to the hospital. I'm eighty-four. I will roll you."

Nonno Beppe sat in his wooden chair with a glass of red wine and a cat on his lap. The cat was named Garibaldi. Garibaldi was very fat and had never fought for anything in his life except the last piece of fish.

"Nonno, the internet is broken."

"The internet has been broken since they invented it."

"No, I mean it doesn't *work*. The man from the company says a week. A *week*, Nonno. Seven days. What am I supposed to do for seven days?"

From the kitchen, Nonna Rosa shouted: "You could help me with the dishes!"

Luca sat down on the porch step very quickly. "Tell me a story, Nonno."

Nonno Beppe smiled the slow smile of a man who has been waiting fourteen years for exactly this moment.

"Va bene. One story every night until the Wi-Fi comes back. But I warn you. They are true stories. Some will make you laugh. Some will make you cry. And all of them are about people who said: *per la patria morii io*. For my homeland, I died."

"That's dark, Nonno."

"Life is a little dark, figlio mio. That's why God made candles." He lifted his wine glass. "And wine."

"*BEPPE!*" shouted Nonna Rosa.

"And *water*," said Nonno Beppe quickly. "God also made water."

---

## CHAPTER ONE: The Girl Who Told the Generals to Go to Confession

"The first story," said Nonno Beppe, "is about a girl younger than your cousin Chiara."

"Chiara is seventeen and she cries when her nail polish chips."

"This girl was also seventeen. She did not cry about nail polish. She cried for her country."

---

Her name was Marie, and she lived in a tiny village where nothing happened except sheep.

Her country was at war, and the war had been going on so long that nobody could remember what peace looked like. Foreign armies held half the land. The rightful prince had never been crowned. He spent his days in a castle feeling sorry for himself and eating very good cheese.

Marie was a farm girl. She could not read. She could spin wool better than anyone in the village, and she prayed so much that the village priest once asked her, kindly, to leave the church so he could lock it.

When she was thirteen, Marie began to hear voices.

"Uh oh," said Luca.

"Not *that* kind of voices," said Nonno Beppe. "She said they were saints. And the saints told her something terrible: *Marie, go and save your country.*"

Marie said what any sensible teenage girl would say.

"Me? I'm a girl. I can't ride a horse. I don't know how to fight. I've never even been to the next town."

And the voices said, more or less: *Yes. That's the point.*

---

When she was seventeen, Marie walked to the nearest army captain and told him that God had sent her to save the kingdom.

The captain laughed so hard he had to sit down. He told her cousin to take her home and give her a good slap.

Marie came back. And came back. And came back again. She was so stubborn that the captain finally gave her a horse, a sword, and some men's clothes, mostly so she would go and bother somebody else.

She rode eleven days through enemy country to see the prince. The prince, to test her, hid among his courtiers and put another man on his throne in fancy clothes.

Marie walked into the great hall, looked at the fake prince on the throne, ignored him completely, walked straight to the real prince hiding in the crowd, and bowed.

The whole room went silent.

"How did she know?" asked Luca.

"Maybe God told her. Maybe the real prince just had a very guilty face. Either way, he gave her an army."

---

Now here is the funny part. Marie's soldiers were not saints. They swore. They drank. They gambled. They did other things that I will not describe because your Nonna is listening.

"I AM LISTENING," said Nonna Rosa from the kitchen.

Marie was disgusted. She was seventeen and she marched into a camp of rough, smelly, foul-mouthed soldiers and announced:

*No more swearing. Everybody goes to confession. Everybody goes to Mass. And all of you, take a bath.*

The soldiers looked at the generals. The generals looked at each other. One of the generals, a big, scary man who swore more than anyone in the army, tried very hard to stop. Marie let him swear by his walking stick instead of by God. So for the rest of the war, this terrifying warrior would get angry and shout, "BY MY STICK!"

Luca laughed so hard that Garibaldi the cat jumped off Nonno's lap in offence.

"And the strange thing," said Nonno Beppe, "is that it worked. The army that had lost for years began to win. Marie carried a banner instead of a sword, because she said she never wanted to kill anyone. She was shot through the shoulder with an arrow, pulled it out, cried a little, prayed, and went back to the fight. They broke the siege of a great city. Then another city. Then she led the prince to the cathedral where kings were crowned, and she stood beside him with her banner while they put the crown on his head."

"So she won! Happy ending!"

Nonno Beppe was quiet for a moment.

"No, figlio mio. Not yet."

---

The year after, Marie was captured in battle. She was nineteen.

Her enemies sold her to the foreign army like a sack of grain. And the prince she had crowned, the prince she had saved, did nothing. He did not pay. He did not send an army. He did not send a letter.

They put her on trial. Learned men, old men, men with big hats and big books, asked her trick questions for months. They tried to make her say something foolish so they could call her a heretic.

One of them asked her: *Are you in the grace of God?*

It was a trap. If she said yes, she was proud. If she said no, she was confessing she was guilty.

The farm girl who could not read answered:

*"If I am not, may God put me there. And if I am, may God keep me there."*

The learned men had nothing to say.

"That's actually really smart," said Luca.

"The smartest answer in the whole trial. And still they condemned her."

They burned her in the marketplace of a foreign city. She asked for a cross. There was none. An enemy soldier, a rough man who had probably sworn a thousand times, felt something break inside him. He picked up two sticks, tied them together, and gave them to her. She held that little cross against her heart.

A priest ran to the church and brought back a real crucifix and held it up high so she could see it through the smoke.

The last word she said was the name of Jesus.

Some of the soldiers who watched went home crying. One of them said: *We are lost. We have burned a saint.*

---

Luca didn't say anything for a while.

"Nonno, the prince didn't even help her."

"No."

"That's not fair."

"No. It wasn't. People who give everything are not always thanked. Sometimes they're forgotten by the very people they saved." Nonno Beppe scratched Garibaldi behind the ears. "But God does not forget. Not one of them."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the First Night
> **"Let no one despise you for your youth, but set the believers an example in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity."** *(1 Timothy 4:12)*
>
> Marie was young, poor, and could not read. Everyone thought she was the wrong person. God often chooses "the wrong person" so that everyone can see the strength comes from Him, not from us. You do not need to be old, rich, or important to be brave. You need to say yes.
>
> And when the people you helped forget you, remember: you did not do it for their thanks. You did it for God, and He keeps perfect records.

---

## CHAPTER TWO: The Worst Spy in History

The second night, the Wi-Fi was still dead. Luca had tried turning the router off and on fourteen times. Nonna Rosa had tried blessing it with holy water. Nothing.

"Tonight," said Nonno Beppe, "a story about a schoolteacher."

"A teacher? Nonno, teachers don't die for their country. They die of *boredom*. From *us*."

"This one was twenty-one years old. Tall, handsome, very clever, a champion at jumping. He could jump out of a barrel. Standing inside a barrel, feet together, and *hop*, out."

"Why would you need to do that?"

"Nobody knows. But the girls liked it."

---

His name was Ethan, and he taught school in a small town in a land across the ocean. His country was not even a country yet. It was a group of colonies that had decided they were tired of being ruled by a king who lived very far away and kept sending them taxes.

When the war for independence began, Ethan closed his schoolbooks and became a soldier. His students were sad. Some of them were older than him, which made class very confusing.

In the second year of the war, things were going terribly. The enemy army was enormous. The general of Ethan's army needed someone to sneak behind enemy lines, find out where they would attack, and come back alive.

The general asked for a volunteer.

Nobody moved.

Ethan raised his hand.

His friend grabbed his arm and whispered: *Are you crazy? Spies are not soldiers. If they catch a soldier, they put him in prison. If they catch a spy, they hang him. There is no honour in it. They will call you a criminal.*

Ethan said: *Every kind of service necessary to the public good becomes honourable by being necessary.*

"Okay," said Luca, "that's a good line."

"Wait. Now comes the part where he is the worst spy in history."

---

Ethan disguised himself as a schoolteacher.

"...He *was* a schoolteacher."

"Exactly. Not a very creative disguise. He took his real diploma from his real university with his real name on it. He told everybody he was a schoolteacher looking for work, in the middle of a war zone, where there were no schools."

Luca put his face in his hands.

"Also, he had a big scar on his face from a gunpowder accident, so he was very easy to recognise. Also, he was terrible at lying. He was an honest man. Honest men make bad spies. When people asked him questions, he blushed."

"Nonno, this is like a comedy movie."

"It is! For a few days, it really is. He wandered around enemy camps, drawing maps and taking notes and hiding them in the soles of his shoes. He actually got good information. He was nearly home."

Nonno Beppe stopped smiling.

"And then he trusted the wrong man."

---

In a tavern, a stranger sat down beside him. Friendly. Talkative. The stranger said he was secretly on Ethan's side too. He said he was also a spy. He bought Ethan dinner. Ethan, who was honest and therefore believed other people were honest, talked too much.

The next morning, enemy soldiers were waiting for him. They pulled off his shoes and found the maps.

There was no trial. There was no judge. The enemy general simply ordered him hanged the next morning.

That night, Ethan asked for two things.

He asked for a chaplain, a minister to pray with him. They said no.

He asked for a Bible. They said no.

He wrote letters to his mother and his brother. The officer in charge read them, laughed, and tore them up in front of him, so that nobody would ever know he had died bravely.

"That's evil," said Luca quietly.

"Yes. But something happened that the evil man didn't plan. There was another enemy officer there, an engineer, a decent man. He let Ethan sit in his tent while they prepared the rope. He gave him paper and ink. He watched how calm this young man was. He was so moved that later, under a flag of truce, he crossed over to Ethan's side and told them everything. He told them that their schoolteacher had died like a gentleman."

"What did he say? At the end?"

Nonno Beppe set down his glass. When he spoke, his voice was very soft.

"He looked at the soldiers. He looked at the crowd. He was twenty-one. He said:

*I only regret that I have but one life to lose for my country.*"

The cicadas sang in the dark. Garibaldi the cat, for once, did not move.

"He failed his mission, Nonno."

"Yes. He never brought the maps home. As a spy, he failed completely." Nonno Beppe pointed a finger at Luca. "But as a man, he did not fail at all. And in the end, which one do you think God cares about?"

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Second Night
> **"Moreover, it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful."** *(1 Corinthians 4:2)*
>
> God does not ask us to be successful. He asks us to be faithful. Ethan was clumsy, too honest, and he trusted the wrong man. His plan failed. But he did not run, he did not beg, and he did not betray anyone.
>
> When they took away his Bible and tore up his letters, they thought they could erase him. But you cannot erase someone God remembers. Do the right thing, even if you do it badly. God can work with clumsy. He cannot work with cowardly.

---

## CHAPTER THREE: The Tooth-Puller

On the third night, Luca came out to the porch carrying two glasses of lemonade and a plate of Nonna's biscotti without being asked.

Nonno Beppe narrowed his eyes. "What do you want?"

"Nothing! I just want the story."

"Mamma mia. Three days without internet and the boy becomes a saint."

---

"Tonight," said Nonno, "we go to a land of mountains and gold, very far across the ocean, under the rule of a small kingdom in Europe that took that gold and sent it home on ships. Lots and lots of gold. And the people who dug it out of the ground stayed poor."

"That sounds unfair."

"It was. And there was a man there called Antônio. Antônio had many jobs, because he could never make enough money at one. He was a soldier, a junior officer. He was a miner. He was a merchant. He was an engineer who had ideas about bringing water to the city. And in his free time..." Nonno Beppe bared his teeth, "he pulled teeth."

"Like a dentist?"

"Like a dentist, but with no school, no anaesthetic, and a pair of pliers. People came to him with a toothache, he sat them on a chair, put his knee on their chest, and *pop!* He also carved false teeth out of bone to put in the gaps. He was very good at it. So everybody called him by a nickname. They called him *Tooth-Puller*."

Luca winced and touched his jaw. "I would rather die."

"Some of his patients felt the same."

---

Antônio talked a lot. While he pulled your tooth, you couldn't answer, so he talked even more. And what he talked about was freedom.

*Why should our gold go across the sea? Why do we pay taxes to a queen we have never seen? Other people across the ocean have made their own country. Why not us?*

He found friends who agreed: poets, priests, lawyers, rich men. They met secretly and made plans. They even designed a flag. They planned to rise up on the day the government came to collect a huge new tax.

"So what happened?"

"What always happens when you plan a revolution with too many people," said Nonno Beppe. "One of them had very big debts. And he realised he could make them disappear."

He went to the governor and betrayed them all.

---

The soldiers arrested everyone. The poets, the priests, the lawyers, the rich men. The trial went on for almost three years.

And during the trial, a strange thing happened.

The rich men and the important men began to say: *It wasn't me. I was only listening. It was all his idea. The tooth-puller.*

"Cowards," said Luca.

"Wait. Here is the strange part. When the judges asked Antônio, he did not say *it was them*. He said: *Yes. It was me. I was the leader. Punish me. Let them go.*"

Luca stared at him.

"In the end, the judges condemned eleven men to death. Then, on the day the sentence was read, the queen sent a letter. She would show mercy. All of them would be sent into exile instead.

All of them... except one."

"Antônio."

"Antônio. The poorest. The least important. The one with no rich family to protect him. They needed to make an example, and they chose the tooth-puller."

---

"Was he angry?"

"The friars who stayed with him in prison said he was the calmest man in the building. He told them he was happy, because his death would save the lives of the others. He prayed. He walked to the gallows holding a crucifix, through streets full of soldiers, because they were afraid the people would riot."

Nonno Beppe paused.

"Then they did a terrible thing. After he died, they cut his body into pieces and hung the pieces along the road, where he used to travel, so that everybody would see what happens to people who talk about freedom. They salted the ground where his house stood so nothing would ever grow there."

Luca was pale. "Why would anyone do that?"

"Because they were afraid of him. You don't do that to a man you think is unimportant. You do that to a man you know people will remember." Nonno Beppe smiled sadly. "And they were right. Today, in that country, the day he died is a national holiday. His face is on statues and money. The man who pulled teeth for coins became the father of his nation."

"And the guy who betrayed them?"

"Nobody names their children after *him*."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Third Night
> **"But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him."** *(Isaiah 53:5)*
>
> When the important men pointed at Antônio and said "it was him," he did not point back. He took the whole weight on his own shoulders so that others could live. That is a small picture of what Jesus did for us: the innocent One carrying the punishment so the guilty could go free.
>
> When something goes wrong, the world says: *find someone to blame.* Christ says: *find someone to protect.*

---

## CHAPTER FOUR: The Doctor Who Wrote Too Well

On the fourth night, Luca came out with a notebook.

"What's that?"

"I'm writing them down. The stories."

Nonno Beppe pretended he had something in his eye.

---

"Tonight's man," said Nonno Beppe, "was the cleverest person I have ever heard of. And I once met a man in Naples who could open any car with a spoon."

His name was Emilio, and he was born on a group of islands in Asia ruled by a kingdom in Europe. Most of the land belonged to powerful religious orders and to foreigners. The local people were treated like children who could not be trusted with anything.

Emilio was not a child who could not be trusted with anything.

By the time he grew up, he was an eye doctor. He was also a poet, a novelist, a painter, a sculptor, an engineer, a farmer, a fencer, and a teacher. He spoke more than ten languages. He studied in Europe, in the great universities. He made sculptures out of clay that won prizes. He once fought off a pirate... no, I made that up. But he *could* have.

"Nonno!"

"The rest is true! He went home and performed surgery on his own mother's eyes, because she was going blind, and she could see again. Imagine. 'Mamma, hold still, I'm operating.'"

---

But Emilio had a problem. He could not stop seeing what was wrong in his country. And worse: he could write about it beautifully.

He wrote a novel. In it, he described the corrupt officials, the greedy landlords, and the cruel men who called themselves holy but were not. He was careful. He wrote with humour. His villains were so ridiculous they were funny.

The powerful men read it, recognised themselves, and were not amused.

"Oh no."

"Oh yes. They banned the book. Anyone caught reading it could be punished. So, of course, *everybody* read it."

Luca grinned. "Like when Nonna says don't eat the cake, so you eat the cake."

"Exactly like the cake."

---

Emilio wrote a second novel, even sharper. Then he came home, and the government exiled him to a tiny town at the far end of the islands.

Do you know what he did there?

He built a school. He built a water system for the whole town using bamboo pipes. He opened a clinic and treated poor people for free. He drained marshes. He collected rare insects and sent them to museums in Europe, and three species of animals were named after him. He taught the local boys reading, maths, and how to defend themselves.

"In *exile*?"

"In exile. Some men, you put them in a cage and they complain. Some men, you put them in a cage and they build a better cage. And then a school. And then a hospital."

---

But the islands were boiling. A revolution began, a violent one. Emilio had never wanted violence. He believed his people should first be educated, should first learn to rule themselves wisely, before taking up guns.

It didn't matter. The government needed someone to blame, and the man who wrote the books was the obvious choice. They arrested him, put him on trial, and sentenced him to death by firing squad.

The night before he died, he wrote a long poem. A farewell to his country. He folded it very small and hid it inside a little alcohol lamp, and when his sister visited, he whispered to her in English, which the guards didn't understand: *There is something in the lamp.*

"Like a secret message!"

"The most famous poem in his country's history. Hidden in a lamp."

---

In the morning, they marched him to the field by the sea.

The soldiers told him to turn his back to the firing squad. That is how traitors were shot. In the back.

Emilio said: *I am not a traitor. Let me face you.*

They refused.

A doctor came and took his pulse before the execution. The doctor was amazed. It was completely normal. A man about to be shot, and his heart was as calm as if he was taking a walk.

When the rifles fired, Emilio, with the last strength in his body, twisted around as he fell, so that he landed facing the sky.

His last words were the last words of Jesus on the cross: *"Consummatum est."* It is finished.

---

Nonno Beppe recited, slowly, a few lines he said he'd learned as a young man:

*"I die just when I see the dawn break,*
*through the gloom of night, to herald the day..."*

"That's from the lamp poem?"

"From the lamp poem."

Luca wrote it carefully in his notebook. He did not look at his phone once that night.

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Fourth Night
> **"Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good."** *(Romans 12:21)*
>
> Emilio fought injustice with a pen, a scalpel, a school, and bamboo water pipes, never with hatred. When they exiled him, he served the people around him. When they shot him, he refused to be called a traitor, because he wasn't one.
>
> Christians are called to tell the truth even when it costs us, and to tell it with love. Being right is not enough. You must also be good. The best answer to people who do evil is not to become like them, but to build a school right next door.

---

## CHAPTER FIVE: The Nurse, the Dog, and the Secret Soldiers

On the fifth night, it rained. They sat inside, at the kitchen table, while Nonna Rosa made coffee and pretended she wasn't listening. Garibaldi slept on the warm top of the useless Wi-Fi router.

"At least it's good for something," said Nonno Beppe.

---

"Tonight's story is about a woman," said Nonno. "A nurse. Very strict. Very serious. The kind of nurse who, if you said 'it doesn't hurt,' would look at you over her glasses until you admitted that it hurt."

"Like Nonna."

"*I heard that,*" said Nonna Rosa.

"Exactly like Nonna. Her name was Ellen. She was the daughter of a poor country priest in a cold, wet little village, and she grew up believing one simple thing: if you see someone suffering, you help them. You don't ask who they are. You don't ask what flag they wave. You help."

---

Ellen became a nurse, and then the head of a nursing school in a big foreign city. She was very good at her job. She was also very particular. Her nurses had to be on time to the second. Their uniforms had to be perfect. Their beds had to be made so tight you could bounce a coin off them.

But Ellen had one weakness.

Dogs.

She had two dogs, and one of them, a scruffy little mutt called Jack, was the worst-behaved animal in the city. He barked at visitors. He growled at doctors. He bit the postman. Everyone was afraid of Jack, except Ellen, who thought Jack was a perfect angel.

Garibaldi opened one eye on top of the router, as if to say: *I understand this Jack.*

---

Then the war came. The biggest war the world had ever seen. Enemy armies marched into the city where Ellen worked and took it over.

Ellen could have gone home to safety. She stayed. Her hospital was full of wounded men, and wounded men need nurses. And she nursed *everyone*. Soldiers from her own country. Soldiers from the enemy army. She cleaned their wounds the same way, gave them the same soup, and was equally strict with all of them.

But soon, other men began to knock at her door at night.

They were soldiers from her country's army, trapped behind enemy lines. Hungry. Hiding. If the enemy found them, they would be shot.

Ellen hid them.

She hid them in the hospital, dressed as patients. She hid them in the basement. Once, when enemy police came to search, she hid a soldier inside a barrel of apples.

"An *apple barrel*?"

"An apple barrel. And she stood next to it and talked to the police very seriously about hospital hygiene until they left."

Luca laughed. "She's like a superhero."

"With a group of brave friends, she secretly smuggled about two hundred soldiers out of the city and across the border to a country that wasn't in the war. Two hundred lives. One strict nurse. And one terrible dog, who barked at everyone, but somehow never barked at the hidden soldiers."

---

Of course, it could not last.

Someone talked. The enemy arrested Ellen and her friends. At her trial, she did something nobody expected.

She told the truth.

She did not lie. She did not deny it. When they asked her if she had helped the soldiers escape, she said yes. When they asked how many, she told them. Her lawyer nearly fainted.

"Why would she do that?"

"Because she was a Christian, and she had decided that she would not save her own life with a lie."

They sentenced her to death.

The whole world was shocked. Diplomats from neutral countries begged for mercy. They said: *She is a nurse. She is a woman. She saved the lives of your soldiers too.* The enemy refused.

---

On her last night, a minister from her own church was allowed to visit her in her cell.

He expected to find her crying. Instead, she was calm. She thanked God for the quiet weeks in prison, because she had finally had time to rest and pray. They shared Holy Communion together. Then, quietly, in the cold cell, they sang an old hymn together: *Abide with me, fast falls the eventide.*

And then she said the words that are now carved in stone beside her statue:

*"Patriotism is not enough. I must have no hatred or bitterness towards anyone."*

Nonna Rosa had stopped pouring coffee. She was standing very still by the stove.

"In the morning," said Nonno Beppe, "they shot her. She was fifty years old. She had spent her whole life healing the bodies of strangers."

---

"What happened to Jack?" asked Luca, very quietly.

"A kind princess, one of Ellen's brave friends, took him in. He lived for many years. He was still badly behaved. And when he died, people loved him so much that they preserved him. You can still see him today, in a museum, in a glass case. Scruffy little Jack, still guarding his lady."

Luca rubbed his eyes. "Stupid rain. Gets in your eyes."

"Yes," said Nonno Beppe, who was rubbing his own. "Stupid rain."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Fifth Night
> **"But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you."** *(Matthew 5:44)*
>
> Ellen loved her country. But she knew that loving your country is not the highest love. "Patriotism is not enough." A Christian loves her country, and also the enemy soldier bleeding in the hospital bed. She would not lie to save her own life, and she would not hate the men who killed her.
>
> It is easy to love people who wave the same flag as you. Jesus asks for more. He asks us to love the people on the other side, and to keep our hearts free of bitterness, even at the very end.

---

## CHAPTER SIX: The Man Who Got Himself Arrested On Purpose

On the sixth night, Luca brought out the lemonade, the biscotti, and a blanket for Nonno's knees, because the sea wind was cold.

"Nonno, is it true that tomorrow the Wi-Fi comes back?"

"So they say."

"Then tomorrow is the last story?"

"Tomorrow is the last story. And it is the most important one. But tonight, I'll tell you about the bravest man I have ever heard of. Braver than all the others."

"Braver than the nurse?"

"Different. Let me ask you a question. If you heard there was a terrible place, a prison where people were starved and beaten and killed, the worst place on earth... what would you do?"

"Stay away from it?"

"Correct. Normal people stay away from it. Tomasz *broke in*."

---

Tomasz was a cavalry officer in a country in the middle of Europe. Before the war, he lived on a farm. He loved horses. He painted pictures. He wrote poems. He had a wife and two small children, and he was the kind of father who made wooden toys by hand and took the children to see the cows.

Then two enormous armies invaded his country from both sides at once, and crushed it.

Tomasz did not surrender. He joined the secret underground army. And soon they heard rumours about a new camp the enemy had built. Thousands of people were being sent there. Not many were coming back. Nobody knew what was really happening inside.

The underground army needed someone to find out.

Tomasz raised his hand.

"Like Ethan the spy!"

"Like Ethan. But a much, much better spy. Because Tomasz had a plan. He took a false name. He waited until the enemy soldiers were rounding up people on the streets. Then he deliberately walked into the street and let himself get caught."

"On *purpose*?!"

"The only man in history who tried his very hardest to get arrested. Imagine the soldiers: 'You there! Stop!' and Tomasz says: 'Yes, of course, thank you, please, where do I go?'"

Luca laughed, but it was a nervous laugh.

---

They put him on a train to the camp. The stories were true. They were worse than true.

Tomasz was given a number instead of a name. He was starved. He was beaten. He was made to work in the freezing cold. He watched men die every day.

And in the middle of all this, he did his job.

He secretly organised the prisoners into little groups. Five men each, so if one group was caught, the others would be safe. They helped each other. They stole extra bread for the sick. They smuggled medicine. They got the weakest men moved into easier jobs. They kept each other alive.

And Tomasz wrote reports. Secret reports about what was really happening in the camp, and smuggled them out to the underground army, and from there to the rest of the world. He was one of the very first people to tell the world the truth about that terrible place.

"How long was he in there?"

"Two and a half years."

"*Years?*"

"And then, when he realised the world was not going to come and save them, he escaped. One night, with two friends, working the night shift in the camp bakery, outside the main fence. They unbolted a door, cut a telephone wire, and ran. They ran and ran until they reached the forest."

Luca let out his breath. "So he lived!"

"He lived. He went back to the fighting. He fought in a great uprising in his capital city. And when the war finally ended, he thought it would be over."

"But?"

"But one of the two armies that had invaded his country never left. They put their own men in charge. And so Tomasz, who could have escaped abroad and lived safely, stayed. He started gathering information again, about the new oppressors. Because his country still wasn't free."

---

This time, he was caught.

The new secret police tortured him for months. They broke his bones. They pulled out his fingernails. When his wife was finally allowed to see him, he told her something terrible, very quietly:

*"Compared to this, that camp was just a game."*

Luca put his lemonade down.

They held a fake trial. They sentenced him to death. And in a prison in his own capital, in his own free-but-not-free country, they shot him in the back of the head. They buried him secretly so nobody could ever visit his grave. For forty years, his name was forbidden. In school, children were not allowed to learn about him.

"But now?"

"Now his country is free. And now they teach his name in every school."

"Nonno... how could he keep going? After all that?"

"He had a small book that he loved. An old Christian book called *The Imitation of Christ*. It is about following Jesus in small things: being humble, carrying your cross, trusting God when everything goes dark. He read it in the war. He wanted his family to read it too." Nonno Beppe shrugged. "Some men get their courage from anger. Tomasz got his from somewhere much deeper."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Sixth Night
> **"Remember them that are in bonds, as bound with them; and them which suffer adversity, as being yourselves also in the body."** *(Hebrews 13:3)*
>
> Most of us run away from suffering. Tomasz walked straight into it, so he could help the people trapped inside, and so the world would know the truth. That is what Jesus did: He did not save us from far away. He came down into our prison, wore our chains, and walked out the other side, opening the door behind Him.
>
> You probably won't be asked to break into a prison. But someone near you is suffering: at school, in your family, in your street. Don't stay away. Walk in. Sit with them. Carry some of the weight.

## CHAPTER SEVEN: The Twenty-Two

On the seventh night, the man from the internet company came at six o'clock in the evening. He fixed the cable. He tested the router. Garibaldi the cat hissed at him from the top of the fridge.

"All working!" said the man. "Full speed!"

Luca's phone buzzed in his pocket. Then it buzzed again. And again. And again. A week of messages, all arriving at once.

He took it out. He looked at it.

Then he turned it off, put it on the kitchen table, and went out to the porch.

Nonno Beppe was already there. But tonight he had no wine. He had a small, old, black-and-white photograph in his hands.

---

"This last story happened here," said Nonno Beppe. "Not in a faraway country. Here. Three kilometres down the road. Near the old tower by the sea."

Luca sat down slowly.

"It was 1943. The war was going very badly for Italy. In September, Italy surrendered, and overnight, the German soldiers, who had been our allies, became our occupiers. They were angry. They felt betrayed. And they were everywhere.

In our little town, there was a police station. And in that police station there was a young *carabiniere*. A policeman. His name was Sandro."

---

Sandro was twenty-two. He came from Naples, which meant he talked with his hands, sang badly in the shower, and believed that no pizza north of Rome was real pizza.

"He's right," said Luca.

"Of course he's right. He was a Neapolitan. They are always right about pizza and wrong about everything else."

Sandro was the junior officer in the station. His commander was away. So suddenly, this skinny kid from Naples was the most important policeman in town. He was in charge of keeping the peace in the middle of a war. He mostly dealt with stolen chickens and arguments over fences.

He was religious. He went to Mass. He was kind to old women and fair to troublemakers. He wrote letters home to his mother. The village liked him.

---

There were some German soldiers camped in the old tower by the sea. On the night of the twenty-second of September, they were going through a box of old ammunition left behind by the army.

Something exploded.

One German soldier was killed. Others were wounded.

It was an accident. Everybody knew it was an accident. But the German commander did not want an accident. He wanted someone to blame. He said: *the villagers did this. This was an attack.*

And the next morning, the Germans went through the village and grabbed men from their homes, from the fields, from the streets. Farmers. Workers. Old men. Young men. Twenty-two of them.

They dragged Sandro along too, because he was the police.

---

They took all of them to the sand near the tower. They gave the twenty-two men shovels. And they made them dig.

"Dig what?" asked Luca.

Nonno Beppe did not answer for a moment.

"Their own grave."

---

The Germans questioned Sandro. They beat him. They demanded: *Who did it? Who are the guilty ones?*

Sandro said: *Nobody did it. It was an accident. These men are innocent.*

They didn't care. The grave was getting deeper. The men were crying, praying, thinking of their wives and children. Some of them were fathers of tiny babies.

And Sandro, twenty-two years old, looked at the hole in the sand. He looked at the twenty-two men. He looked at the German officer.

And he said:

*"It was me. I did it. I am the only one responsible. Let them go."*

---

The German officer looked at him for a long time. Then he gave the order.

The twenty-two men were released. They climbed out of the grave and ran. Some of them looked back. They saw the young policeman standing alone in front of the soldiers, in his uniform, by the hole they had dug.

Some of them remembered that he said, at the very end, something like this:

*"If I die for others, I will be born again a hundred times. God is with me, and I am not afraid."*

Then the rifles fired.

It was the twenty-third of September, 1943.

---

The cicadas had stopped singing. Even the sea seemed quiet.

Luca's voice cracked. "Nonno, he didn't even do anything. He *lied* to save them."

"He took the blame for something nobody did. So that twenty-two families would not lose their fathers." Nonno Beppe held out the photograph. "Look."

It was a young man, standing in a field, squinting into the sun, holding a baby.

"Who is it?"

"That's my father. Your great-grandfather, Pietro Ferraro. He was one of the twenty-two. He was the one digging the hole at the far end, by the rocks."

Luca's mouth fell open.

"And the baby?"

Nonno Beppe smiled, and two tears ran down into the creases of his old face.

"The baby is me. Eleven months old. My father was digging his own grave, and all he could think was that I would grow up without remembering his face." He tapped the photograph. "But I did grow up. And I remember him. I married your Nonna. We had your mother. Your mother had you."

He took Luca's hand.

"Figlio mio. You understand? You are alive because a twenty-two-year-old boy from Naples stood in front of a firing squad and said *it was me*. Every breath you take, every stupid video you watch on that phone, every plate of your Nonna's pasta, it was paid for. Long before you were born."

---

Nonna Rosa came out onto the porch. She didn't say anything. She just sat down next to them and put her arm around both of them. Even Garibaldi came and lay across Luca's feet.

They sat there for a long time, the three of them and the fat cat, looking at the dark shape of the old tower by the sea.

Finally Luca said:

"Nonno. Tomorrow, can you tell me another story?"

"The Wi-Fi is back."

"I know."

Nonno Beppe laughed, and wiped his eyes, and squeezed his grandson's hand.

"Va bene. Tomorrow, another story."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Seventh Night
> **"Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends."** *(John 15:13)*
>
> Sandro was innocent. He took the guilt of a crime that never happened so that twenty-two men could go home. That is the closest picture any of us will ever see of what Jesus did on the cross: the innocent One said *"put it on Me,"* and the guilty walked free.
>
> Every one of us is alive because someone sacrificed for us: parents, grandparents, people we will never meet. And every Christian is alive forever because Christ said *"it was Me."* The only right answer to a gift like that is to live a life worth the price.

## EPILOGUE: Questa È la Loro Storia
### *This Is Their Story*

The names in this book were changed. The people were real. Every one of them gave their life for their homeland. This is who they really were.

---

**Chapter One — "Marie" was SAINT JOAN OF ARC** *(Jeanne d'Arc), France, c. 1412–1431*
A peasant girl from Domrémy who, guided by the voices she believed were Saint Michael, Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret, led the French army to lift the siege of Orléans in 1429 and brought Charles VII to be crowned at Reims. Captured at Compiègne in 1430 and sold to the English, she was tried at Rouen and burned at the stake on 30 May 1431, aged about 19. An English soldier made her a small cross of wood. Her last word was "Jesus." She was declared innocent in 1456 and canonised a saint in 1920. The general who swore "by his stick" was La Hire.

**Chapter Two — "Ethan" was NATHAN HALE**, United States, 1755–1776
A Yale graduate and schoolteacher from Connecticut who became a captain in the Continental Army. He volunteered to spy behind British lines on Long Island, disguised as a schoolmaster. He was captured and hanged in Manhattan on 22 September 1776, aged 21, without a trial. He was denied a Bible and a chaplain, and his farewell letters were destroyed. A British officer, Captain John Montresor, later reported his courage to the Americans. He is remembered for the words: *"I only regret that I have but one life to lose for my country."*

**Chapter Three — "Antônio" was TIRADENTES** *(Joaquim José da Silva Xavier)*, Brazil, 1746–1792
A cavalry ensign, miner, merchant, and amateur dentist; his nickname means "Tooth-Puller." He was a leader of the *Inconfidência Mineira*, a plot for Brazilian independence from Portugal, which was betrayed in 1789 by Joaquim Silvério dos Reis in exchange for having his debts forgiven. Tiradentes took full responsibility. Eleven conspirators were sentenced to death; the Queen commuted all the sentences except his. He was hanged in Rio de Janeiro on 21 April 1792, and his body was quartered and displayed. 21 April is now a national holiday in Brazil, and he is honoured as the Patron of the Brazilian nation.

**Chapter Four — "Emilio" was DR. JOSÉ RIZAL**, the Philippines, 1861–1896
An ophthalmologist, novelist, poet, sculptor and polyglot. He operated on his own mother's eyes. His novels *Noli Me Tángere* and *El Filibusterismo* exposed abuses under Spanish colonial rule and were banned. While exiled to Dapitan (1892–1896), he built a school, a water system and a clinic, and several animal species are named after him. Although he opposed violent revolution, he was convicted of rebellion and shot at Bagumbayan, Manila, on 30 December 1896. He asked to face the firing squad and was refused; he twisted as he fell so that he landed facing the sky. His farewell poem, *Mi último adiós*, was smuggled out inside an alcohol lamp. His reported last words: *"Consummatum est."* He is the national hero of the Philippines.

**Chapter Five — "Ellen" was EDITH CAVELL**, Great Britain, 1865–1915
The daughter of a Norfolk vicar, she was matron of a nursing school in Brussels. During the German occupation in the First World War, she nursed soldiers of both sides and helped about 200 Allied soldiers escape to the neutral Netherlands. Arrested in 1915, she refused to lie at her trial. On the night before her execution, she received Holy Communion from the Reverend Stirling Gahan, and they sang *"Abide with Me."* She told him: *"Patriotism is not enough. I must have no hatred or bitterness towards anyone."* She was shot by a German firing squad on 12 October 1915. Her dog Jack was taken in by her friend Princess Marie de Croÿ, and can still be seen at the Imperial War Museum in London.

**Chapter Six — "Tomasz" was CAPTAIN WITOLD PILECKI**, Poland, 1901–1948
A cavalry officer, landowner, painter and poet. In September 1940 he deliberately got himself caught in a German street roundup in Warsaw, using the false name *Tomasz Serafiński*, so he could be sent to Auschwitz. As prisoner 4859, he organised a resistance network and smuggled out some of the first reports on the camp. He escaped in April 1943 through the camp bakery, fought in the 1944 Warsaw Uprising, and afterwards gathered intelligence on the Communist regime. Arrested in 1947 and tortured, he told his wife that *"Auschwitz was just a game"* compared to it. He was executed by a shot to the back of the head in Warsaw's Mokotów prison on 25 May 1948. His burial place was hidden, and his name was suppressed until he was officially rehabilitated in 1990.

**Chapter Seven — "Sandro" was SALVO D'ACQUISTO**, Italy, 1920–1943
A vice-brigadier of the Carabinieri from Naples, serving at the station in Torrimpietra, near Rome. On 22 September 1943, an explosion of abandoned munitions at the Torre di Palidoro killed a German soldier and wounded others. The Germans blamed the local people and seized 22 civilians, who were forced to dig their own grave. To save them, Salvo D'Acquisto falsely declared himself the only person responsible. The 22 hostages were released, and he was shot on 23 September 1943, aged 22. He was awarded Italy's Gold Medal of Military Valour, and the Catholic Church has opened his cause for sainthood.

### Author's Note

The seven historical stories in this book follow the real lives and deaths of these seven people. Some small details and conversations have been imagined or simplified for storytelling, especially where history records only the outline of a scene.

Nonno Beppe, Nonna Rosa, Luca, Garibaldi the cat, and great-grandfather Pietro Ferraro are fictional. But the twenty-two men freed at Palidoro were real, and so were their families, and so were the children who grew up because a young carabiniere said *"it was me."*

*Per la patria morirono loro. Per noi è morto Cristo.*
*For their homeland, they died. For us, Christ died.*
