# PER LA PATRIA MORII IO
## LIBRO DUE: *Le Notti dei Ribelli*
### *Book Two: The Nights of the Rebels*

*Seven more nights, seven more stories, one grandfather, and one new friend*

---

## A Note Before We Begin

Every chapter in this book is the true story of a real person who fought for their country and died for it, in war, in battle, in prison, or at the hands of those who feared them.

Inside the stories, their names have been changed, so you can meet them as people first: people who got nervous, told jokes, loved their families and made mistakes.

**Their real names are revealed only at the end of the book.** Try to guess who they are before you get there.

Each chapter ends with a short lesson from the Christian faith.

---

## PROLOGUE: The New Boy

It was the second week of summer, and the Wi-Fi was working perfectly.

Luca Ferraro didn't care.

"Nonno," he said, coming out onto the porch, "I brought someone."

Behind him stood a tall, skinny boy with big glasses and a football under his arm. He held out his hand very politely.

"Buonasera, signore. I'm Samuel. Luca's friend from school. My family is from Congo, in Africa. Luca says you tell stories about people who died for their countries." He pushed his glasses up his nose. "He says you made him cry."

"*I did not cry,*" said Luca. "It was raining."

"It was not raining," said Nonna Rosa from the kitchen.

Nonno Beppe looked at Samuel for a long moment. Then he smiled and pointed at the chair beside him.

"Sit, Samuel. You like biscotti?"

"Yes, signore."

"Good. Then you can stay. Garibaldi, move."

Garibaldi the cat, who was fatter than ever, did not move. Samuel sat on the step instead.

"Tonight," said Nonno Beppe, "we start a new set of stories. Last week I told Luca about people who were brave in many ways. This week, I'll tell you about fighters. Rebels. People who picked up a sword, or a pen, or a microphone, and said: *my country will be free, or I will die trying.*"

"And you won't tell us their names?" asked Samuel.

"Not until the end. That's the game. You have to guess."

Samuel's eyes lit up behind his glasses. "I'm *very* good at guessing."

"We'll see," said Nonno Beppe.

---

## CHAPTER ONE: The Queen Who Jumped Off the Wall

"The first story," said Nonno, "is about a little girl who wanted an elephant."

"Same," said Samuel.

---

Her name was Manu, and she grew up in a palace in a hot, dusty land in the south of Asia, a land of tigers and spices and very old kings.

But Manu was not a princess. Her father was just a servant at the court. Her mother died when she was four, so her father took her to work with him, and she grew up running around the palace with the princes. The boys learned to ride horses. Manu learned to ride horses. The boys learned to use swords. Manu learned to use swords, better than the boys. The boys learned to shoot. Manu learned to shoot.

One day, the prince went for a ride on an elephant. Manu asked to come. The prince said: *No. Elephants are not for servant girls.*

Manu put her hands on her hips and said:

*"One day I will have ten elephants. And you won't be riding any of them."*

Luca and Samuel both laughed.

"That's the story people tell, anyway," said Nonno. "And here's the funny thing. She was right."

---

When Manu was a teenager, the king of a small, proud kingdom called the City of the Hills came looking for a wife. He was much older than her. But he heard about the girl who rode like a soldier, and he married her. She became his queen and was given a new, royal name, but in this story, we'll keep calling her Manu. No guessing yet.

For a while, Manu was happy. She had a baby son. But the baby died when he was only four months old. Then the king grew sick. Before he died, he adopted a little boy from his family to be the next king.

And then the foreigners came.

A great trading company from across the sea had been swallowing up the kingdoms of that land one by one. They had a rule. If a king died without a son *of his own blood*, his kingdom went to them. An adopted son did not count.

They sent a letter to Manu: *Your husband is dead. Your adopted son is not a real heir. Your kingdom now belongs to us. Leave the palace. Here is a small pension.*

Manu read the letter in front of the foreign officer. Then she said the words her people still sing today:

*"I will not give up my city."*

---

For a few years, she fought with lawyers and letters. Then the whole land exploded in a great rebellion against the foreigners, and Manu became what she had been training to be since she was a little girl.

She trained her own army. Men and women. She drilled them herself, on horseback, in the hot sun. She turned the palace into a fortress.

When the foreign army came, with cannons, the siege lasted two weeks. The walls were smashed. The enemy poured into the city.

And then Manu did something nobody had ever seen.

She tied her little adopted son onto her back with a cloth. She climbed onto her horse. And, the legend says, she rode straight off the high walls of the fort, jumped down into the darkness, and galloped through the enemy army and out into the night.

"*With a kid on her back?*" said Samuel.

"With a kid on her back. Some historians argue about the jump. Nobody argues that she escaped."

---

She rode more than a hundred miles in one day and night. Her horse died of exhaustion. She found another horse and kept fighting.

She joined the other rebels. She helped them capture a great fortress. For a moment, it looked like they might win.

But the foreign army came again, bigger and stronger. On a hot day in June, near a monastery on a hill, Manu put on a soldier's uniform, took her sword in her hand, and rode out at the front of her cavalry.

She fought until she was surrounded. She was wounded, and wounded again. She was barely thirty years old.

She had asked her loyal men for one thing: *Do not let the enemy take my body.* So they carried her away, and burned her body in the hermitage nearby before the enemy could reach it.

The foreign general who had chased her for months wrote in his report that she was *the most dangerous of all the rebel leaders*. And, he wrote, the bravest.

---

Samuel was quiet. "And the little boy on her back?"

"He survived. He lived a long life, quietly, poor, but alive. Because his mother jumped off a wall for him."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the First Night
> **"And who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"** *(Esther 4:14)*
>
> When Manu was a servant girl learning to ride with the princes, nobody knew why. God did. Every lesson, every fall from a horse, every time she was told "you can't" was preparing her for one day when her people would need her.
>
> You may not know yet why you are learning what you're learning, or why you're going through what you're going through. But God is never wasting your time. He may be preparing you for a moment that hasn't come yet.

---

## CHAPTER TWO: The Sickly Stick

On the second night, Samuel brought his grandmother's *mikate*, little round doughnuts from Congo. Nonna Rosa tasted one, narrowed her eyes, and demanded the recipe. Samuel said it was a family secret. Nonna Rosa said she respected that, and then spent the whole evening trying to bribe him with lasagna.

"Tonight," said Nonno, "a man who was born a slave."

---

His name was François, and he was born on a sugar plantation on a beautiful island in the Caribbean Sea. The island was the richest colony in the world. It produced so much sugar and coffee that half of Europe sweetened its breakfast with it.

And it ran on the blood of half a million enslaved Africans, who were worked, whipped, and starved until they died, and then replaced with more.

François was born small and sick. He was so thin and weak that the other children gave him a nickname. They called him *Sickly Stick*.

"That's mean," said Samuel.

"It's very mean. Children are mean everywhere. But Sickly Stick decided he would not stay sickly. He trained himself. He swam the rivers. He rode the wildest horses on the plantation. By the time he was a young man, he could jump onto a galloping horse, and he was the best horseman on the whole island."

---

François had a godfather, an old man who secretly taught him to read. So in the evenings, after work, Sickly Stick read every book he could find. He read the Bible. He read books about history and war. He read books about medicine, and learned to heal people and animals with herbs.

Because he was so good with horses, he became the coachman for the plantation manager, which was a better life than working in the fields. Later, he was freed. He worked his own small farm. He married. He had children. He went to Mass every Sunday.

He was nearly fifty years old. Most men his age were thinking about sitting on the porch.

"Like you, Nonno."

"Like me, *exactly*. And then, one hot summer night, the enslaved people of the whole island rose up all at once."

---

At first, François watched. Then he joined. And very quickly, everyone noticed something: the old coachman who read books knew more about war than anyone else on the island.

He trained the rebel slaves into a real army, with discipline and order. He forbade his soldiers from murdering prisoners. He won battle after battle, against one great European army after another. He kept breaking through the enemy lines, finding openings that nobody else could see, until people gave him a new name: *The Opening*.

"Sickly Stick became The Opening," said Samuel. "That's so cool."

"He freed the slaves of the whole island. He became its governor. He wrote it a constitution. He brought back the farms and the trade. The man who was once a slave now sat at a desk writing letters to kings."

---

But across the ocean, a short, ambitious general had made himself ruler of the most powerful country in Europe. And that general wanted his sugar back. He wanted his slaves back.

He sent a huge army across the sea, led by his own brother-in-law.

The war was terrible. Then, one day, a French general sent François a friendly letter. *Let us meet,* it said. *Let us talk about peace. Come to dinner.*

"Don't go," said Luca.

"He went. He was an honest man, and he believed in peace. When he arrived, soldiers grabbed him. He was put on a ship and sent to Europe as a prisoner, with his family.

As they pushed him onto the ship, he turned and said something his people never forgot:

*"In overthrowing me, you have cut down only the trunk of the tree of liberty. It will grow back from the roots, for they are many and they are deep."*"

---

They locked him in a stone castle high in the snowy mountains near the border of Switzerland. He had grown up in the tropics. He had never seen snow.

His cell was freezing. The water dripped down the walls. They took away his books, his paper, his uniform. They gave him little food. He wrote letters to the ruler begging for a trial. The ruler never answered.

In April, after a long winter, the guard opened his cell and found him dead, sitting in his chair by the cold fireplace.

"So he lost," said Samuel quietly.

Nonno Beppe held up one finger. "Eight months later, the people of his island defeated the great general's army, and declared their independence. The first free Black republic in the world. The first country ever to be born from a slave revolution." He smiled. "The trunk was cut down. The roots grew back."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Second Night
> **"Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage."** *(Galatians 5:1)*
>
> They called him Sickly Stick. Later, they called him The Opening. God does not see us by the names other people give us. He sees what we can become.
>
> François believed every human being was made in the image of God, and that no person should ever own another. When he died alone in a cold cell, it looked like the end. But what is planted in God's truth does not die. Like a seed, it goes into the ground and comes up again.

---

## CHAPTER THREE: The Poet on the White Horse

On the third night, Samuel and Luca arrived with a guitar.

"We found it in the garage," said Luca.

"It's mine," said Nonno Beppe. "From 1961. Give it here." He tuned it, very slowly, and then strummed an old, simple melody. "You know this song?"

Samuel started humming. Luca started humming. Nonna Rosa, from the kitchen, sang a few words.

"*Everybody* knows this song," said Samuel.

"Yes. And tonight, I'll tell you about the man who wrote the words."

---

His name was Pepe, and he was born on a long green island in the Caribbean, the most beautiful island in the world, if you ask its people, and they will tell you whether you ask or not.

The island belonged to a European kingdom across the sea. Pepe's own parents were from that kingdom. His father was a soldier. But Pepe was born on the island, and he loved it with his whole heart.

When he was sixteen, he wrote a letter to a school friend, scolding him for joining the colonial army. The police found the letter.

"For a *letter*?"

"For a letter. A sixteen-year-old boy was sentenced to six years of hard labour in a stone quarry. They put a chain on his ankle. He broke rocks under the hot sun until his hands bled and his eyes were damaged by the white dust. For the rest of his life, he wore a ring made from the iron of that chain."

---

After a while, they released him and sent him away into exile.

And for the next twenty-five years, Pepe lived everywhere *except* his island. Spain. Mexico. Guatemala. Venezuela. And, for fifteen years, in New York City, in the cold, in a tiny office, writing.

He wrote poems. He wrote newspaper articles for half of Latin America. He wrote speeches. He even wrote a whole magazine for children, full of stories and lessons, because he said that the children of today are the free people of tomorrow.

He was small, thin, with a big moustache and a black suit he wore so often it went shiny. He had no money. He forgot to eat. He talked so fast and so beautifully that factory workers would sit for hours listening to him, and then empty their pockets to give money to the cause of freedom.

"So he was a writer. Not a fighter."

"That's what everybody thought. That's what *he* thought, for a long time. He organised everything. He united all the exiles who were always fighting each other. He raised the money. He bought the guns. He planned the war. And then the war began."

---

Pepe could have stayed in New York. Everybody told him to. *You're the brain,* they said. *You're the voice. We need you alive.*

But Pepe said: *How can I ask young men to die for our island, if I'm not willing to go with them?*

So in April, Pepe and a few companions sailed in a small boat through a stormy night and landed on a rocky beach on his island. He was forty-two. He had not touched its soil in years. He knelt down and kissed the ground.

The old generals laughed at him a little. This skinny poet in a city suit, trying to march through the jungle. But Pepe never complained. He carried his own pack. He wrote letters by candlelight. He was happier than he had ever been.

---

A few weeks later, the rebels met the enemy army near a place where two rivers meet.

The general ordered Pepe to stay at the back. *You are too important,* he said.

Pepe didn't listen.

He climbed onto a white horse. And with one young soldier beside him, he charged straight toward the enemy lines, galloping through the long grass.

It was the first battle of his life.

Three bullets hit him. He fell from the white horse into the grass between the two rivers. He was dead before anyone could reach him.

---

Samuel had stopped humming.

"He died in his first battle?"

"His very first. Many people said it was foolish. Maybe it was. But he had written once, years before, that he wanted to die not in the darkness, like a traitor, but facing the sun." Nonno strummed the guitar again, softly. "Seven years later, his island was free. And today, every child on that island knows his poems. And the whole world sings his words, without even knowing who wrote them."

He played the melody one more time. The boys sang along.

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Third Night
> **"Therefore if thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink: for in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head. Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good."** *(Romans 12:20–21)*
>
> Pepe was chained and beaten as a boy. He could have spent his life hating the people who did it. Instead, he wrote one of his most famous poems: that even for the cruel man who tears out his heart, he would not grow thorns and nettles, he would grow a *white rose*.
>
> He fought for freedom, but he refused to fight with hatred. And he would not ask others to make a sacrifice he was not willing to make himself. A true leader goes first.

---

## CHAPTER FOUR: The Girl With the Red Hair

On the fourth night, Samuel came early, and he was excited.

"Nonno Beppe, I figured out the first one! The queen with the elephants! I searched it. I *know* who she is!"

"Don't tell me. Don't tell Luca. Write it on a paper and put it in your pocket. At the end of the week, we'll see."

Samuel wrote something on a paper, folded it very small, and put it in his pocket. Luca looked deeply suspicious.

---

"Tonight," said Nonno, "a story from the last war. The big one. The one my father lived through. And it's about a shy girl who wanted to be a lawyer."

Her name was Johanna, and she lived in a flat, windy country in the north of Europe, a country of canals and windmills and millions and millions of bicycles. She was tall and quiet, wore round glasses, and had beautiful red hair. She liked books, and she wanted to help people. So she went to university to study law, because she believed in justice.

"So far, very boring," said Luca.

"So far. Then the army of the cruel dictator invaded her country."

---

At first, life seemed almost normal. But then the new rulers began to make new rules. Johanna's best friends at university were Jewish. One day, they were not allowed to study any more. Then they were not allowed to go to the park. Then they had to wear a yellow star on their coats.

Then the university gave every student a paper to sign. It said: *I promise to be loyal to the new rulers and never act against them.*

If you signed, you could keep studying. If you didn't, you were thrown out of university, and maybe sent to work in a factory in the enemy's country.

The shy girl with the glasses read the paper. Then she handed it back, unsigned.

"*That's* when she stopped being boring," said Samuel.

---

Johanna joined the secret resistance. At first, she did small jobs. She stole identity cards so her Jewish friends could hide under false names. She carried secret newspapers inside her bicycle bag. She helped find hiding places in attics and cellars.

But the resistance needed more. And the shy girl with the glasses learned something about herself: she had nerves of steel.

She learned to shoot a pistol. She rode her bicycle through the city with weapons hidden in her bag, smiling at German soldiers at the checkpoints. She was given dangerous missions against the worst traitors, the ones who were betraying hidden families to the secret police.

The secret police began to hear about her. They didn't know her name. They only knew one thing: *look for the girl with the red hair.*

"Uh oh."

"So Johanna went to the chemist, bought black hair dye, and dyed her famous red hair jet black. She changed her glasses. She became nobody."

"Smart."

"She was very smart. She escaped them for a long time."

---

But in the spring of the last year of the war, at a checkpoint, the soldiers stopped her and searched her bicycle bag. They found the secret newspapers, and a pistol.

In prison, as her black dye grew out, her roots slowly turned red again. And the secret police realised who they had caught.

"Oh no."

The war was almost over. The enemy army was collapsing everywhere. Everybody knew they had lost. The Allied armies were only a few weeks away.

But in the very last days, the secret police took Johanna out to the sand dunes by the sea.

The man with the gun fired, but the shot only grazed her head. She was wounded. She was standing up.

And she looked at him and said:

*"I shoot better than that."*

Then another man fired again, and she died there, in the sand dunes.

It was three weeks before her country was set free.

---

Luca whispered, "*Three weeks*?"

"Three weeks. When the war ended, they searched the dunes and found her body. They gave her a funeral. The Queen of her country herself came. And today there is a statue of her in her city, a girl on a bicycle, so that every child who passes by can see what courage looks like."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Fourth Night
> **"Have not I commanded thee? Be strong and of a good courage; be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed: for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest."** *(Joshua 1:9)*
>
> Johanna's courage began with one small thing: she refused to sign a piece of paper. The great courage of her last day began with the small courage of her first "no."
>
> Most of us will never face a firing squad. But we face little papers every day: the group chat that is cruel to someone, the joke we're supposed to laugh at, the lie that everybody is telling. Practise saying "no" in small things. That is where great courage is born.

---

## CHAPTER FIVE: The Man Who Studied Dirt

On the fifth night, Samuel's mother came to pick him up early, so Nonno Beppe started before the sun had even set.

"Tonight," he said, "a hero who was a scientist. And not a fancy scientist. He studied dirt."

"Dirt?" said Luca.

"Dirt. Soil. Mud. Plants. Rain. Fertiliser. He was an *agronomist*. He could pick up a handful of soil, smell it, and tell you exactly what would grow there."

"That is the least heroic thing I've ever heard," said Samuel.

Nonno Beppe smiled. "That's what his enemies thought too."

---

His name was Abel, and he was born in a small country on the west coast of Africa, a land of rice fields and mangrove swamps and rivers that ran into the sea. His parents came from a group of dry, windy islands in the ocean nearby. Both lands belonged to the same European empire, which had held them for hundreds of years and had built almost no schools.

But Abel got into school. He was brilliant. He won a scholarship to study in the capital of the empire, across the sea. He studied agronomy. He wrote poems. He met other students from the colonies, and they spent long nights talking about one thing: *how do we make our countries free?*

---

When he came home, the colonial government gave him a job. They asked him to do an agricultural census: to travel around the whole country and count the farms, measure the fields, test the soil, and write down how many cows and rice paddies there were.

"Boring," said Luca.

"The government thought so too. That's why they gave it to him. But think about it. For two years, Abel walked through every village in the whole country. He sat with every farmer. He ate in their homes. He learned their languages. He learned who was hungry and why, who was angry and why, which villages trusted each other and which didn't."

Samuel sat up. "He was making a map. For a revolution."

"He was making the best map of his country that anyone had ever made. And he was making friends in every village."

---

A few years later, Abel and a handful of friends founded a secret movement for independence. At first, they tried peaceful protests. But when dockworkers went on strike for better pay, the colonial police opened fire and killed dozens of them.

After that, Abel said: *They have left us no choice.*

The war for independence lasted ten years.

And here is what made Abel different. He did not want an army of angry men with guns. He said: *We are not fighting against the people of the empire. We are fighting against the colonial system.* He made his fighters treat prisoners well. In every area his army freed, he built schools, clinics, and markets before anything else.

His soldiers had to learn to read. Abel said a fighter who couldn't read was only half a fighter.

"He made them do *homework*? During a *war*?"

"He made them do homework during a war."

---

Abel had one rule that he repeated again and again to his people:

*"Tell no lies. Claim no easy victories."*

He meant: don't lie to the people. Don't pretend we're winning when we're not. Don't promise things we cannot give. Tell them the truth, even when it's hard, and they will trust you.

Slowly, slowly, his army freed most of the country. The great empire sent more soldiers, more planes, more bombs, and it still couldn't win.

---

But the empire had another weapon: betrayal.

In the end, it was not enemy soldiers who killed Abel. It was men from his own movement, some of them working with the enemy. One night in January, as he came home from a reception with his wife, a group of them surrounded his car. They tried to tie him up and take him away.

Abel refused. He told them: *If there is a problem, let us talk about it.*

They shot him.

He was forty-eight years old.

---

"Did the country become free?"

"Eight months later, his movement declared independence. A year after that, the empire itself had a revolution at home, and gave up all its colonies. His half-brother became the first president of the new country."

Samuel shook his head. "He walked all those villages. And he didn't get to see it."

"No. Like Moses. He led his people right up to the edge of the promised land, and then God took him home."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Fifth Night
> **"Wherefore putting away lying, speak every man truth with his neighbour: for we are members one of another."** *(Ephesians 4:25)*
>
> "Tell no lies. Claim no easy victories." That could be a verse from the Bible. Abel won the trust of an entire nation not by giving big speeches, but by walking to every village, listening to every farmer, and always telling the truth.
>
> God often uses the "boring" jobs to prepare us for great things. Abel's revolution started with counting cows. Whatever small job you've been given, do it faithfully, listen to people, and never lie to them. That's how trust is built, one village at a time.

---

## CHAPTER SIX: The Big Fellow on the Bicycle

On the sixth night, Samuel arrived with a bandage on his knee.

"What happened?"

"Luca and I had a race on bicycles."

"I won," said Luca.

"You *cheated*."

"Perfect," said Nonno Beppe. "Tonight's hero also loved bicycles. And he also cheated at wrestling."

---

His name was Michael, and he was born on a farm on a green, rainy island off the coast of Europe, an island of sheep, stone walls, and songs. For seven hundred years, that island had been ruled by the big kingdom next door, just across the water.

Michael was the youngest of eight children. He was big, loud, strong, funny, and had a terrible temper. He loved to wrestle. When he was bored, he would grab one of his friends and wrestle them onto the floor until they surrendered. His favourite move was to bite their ear.

"He *bit* people?"

"He bit people. His friends called it 'getting a bit of ear.' He was a very important revolutionary, and he bit people's ears. History is strange."

---

When he was fifteen, Michael went to work in the big kingdom's capital city as a post office clerk. He counted stamps and sorted letters and was very good at numbers.

And he spent all his free time with other young men from his island, talking about freedom.

When he was twenty-five, he went home to join a rebellion. They took over the main post office in the capital. They held it for a week. Then they were crushed. The leaders were executed. Michael was sent to a prison camp.

In the camp, he learned something important: *we can't win by standing in one building waiting to be surrounded. We need to be invisible.*

---

When he got out, Michael became the head of intelligence for the rebel army. He built a network of spies everywhere: in hotels, in post offices, in police stations, in the enemy's own headquarters. He knew what the enemy was going to do before the enemy did.

The enemy put a huge reward on his head. Posters everywhere. *Wanted. Dead or alive.*

And what did Michael do?

He rode his bicycle.

He rode his bicycle all over the capital city, in broad daylight, in a nice suit, whistling. He said hello to policemen. He went to the same restaurants, the same hotels, the same barber. He was so confident and so ordinary-looking that nobody believed the most wanted man in the empire would just cycle past them.

"That's genius," said Samuel.

"Once, the soldiers raided a house where he was hiding. He climbed out of the skylight onto the roof, in his socks, and walked away across the rooftops. Another time, soldiers stopped him at a checkpoint and searched him. He made jokes with them until they let him go."

---

After two years of war, the big empire asked for peace talks. And the rebel leaders sent Michael to negotiate.

He was thirty years old. He sat at the table with the most famous, most experienced politicians in the world. Old men. Clever men. One of them was a man who would later become one of the most famous war leaders in history.

For weeks, they argued. Every morning, before the negotiations, Michael went to Mass.

In the end, the empire offered a deal. Not complete freedom. But most of it. A free state with its own government, its own army, its own laws. Only part of the island would stay with the empire.

Michael knew many of his own comrades would hate it. They wanted everything. But he also knew that if they said no, the empire would send a huge army, and thousands would die.

He called it *the freedom to achieve freedom*. A first step.

He signed. And as he signed, he said to a friend:

*"I may have signed my actual death warrant."*

---

He was right.

His own country split in two over the treaty. Old friends became enemies. Brothers fought brothers. A civil war began between the people who accepted the peace and the people who refused it.

Michael became the commander of the new army of the new free state. But he hated fighting his old friends. He kept trying to make peace.

In August, he went on a tour of his home county, near the farm where he was born. His friends warned him it was dangerous. He said: *They won't shoot me in my own county.*

On a quiet country road, in a valley whose name means "the mouth of the flowers," his convoy was ambushed. There was a gunfight in the evening light. Michael stood up to fire back.

A single bullet hit him in the head.

He was thirty-one years old.

---

"His own people killed him," said Luca.

"Yes. That's the saddest kind of war, figlio mio. When people who used to be brothers start shooting at each other." Nonno Beppe sighed. "Half a million people came to his funeral. Even some of the men who had fought against him wept. Because they knew. He had chosen peace, even though it cost him his life."

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Sixth Night
> **"Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God."** *(Matthew 5:9)*
>
> Michael was a fighter, but in the end, the bravest thing he did was not fighting. It was choosing peace when everyone around him wanted more war. He knew it might cost him his life, and he did it anyway.
>
> Making peace is often harder than making war. Peacemakers get attacked from both sides. But Jesus says they will be called children of God. When there's a fight among your friends, your family or your class, be the one who brings people back together, even if no one thanks you.

## CHAPTER SEVEN: The Beer Salesman Who Woke Up a Nation

On the last night, Samuel came alone, and he was quiet. He sat on the step and didn't touch the biscotti.

"Samuel, what's wrong?" asked Luca.

"My nonna said something. She said tonight's story might be about our country."

Nonno Beppe nodded slowly. "Your nonna is a wise woman. Tonight's story is about your country. And I'll tell it to you the way your nonna would want it told."

---

His name was Emery, and he was born in a small village in the middle of Africa, in the heart of the great green forest, in a land so big that you could fit all of Western Europe inside it.

That land belonged to the king of a small European country, a king who had once owned it *personally*, like a private farm. Under that king's rule, millions of people had died, forced to collect rubber in the forest. If they didn't collect enough, the king's soldiers cut off their hands.

"*What?*" said Luca.

Samuel didn't look surprised. "My nonna told me," he said quietly.

"Later, the king was forced to give the land to his country's government," said Nonno. "Things improved a little. But only a little. The Africans were still treated like children. There were almost no African doctors, no African lawyers, no African officers. When independence finally came, there were fewer than twenty university graduates in a country of fourteen million people."

---

Emery was clever, and he went to the mission schools. Some Protestant, some Catholic. He read everything. He wrote poems. He got a job as a clerk in the post office.

"Like Michael!"

"Like Michael. Then he became a beer salesman."

Luca burst out laughing. "A *beer salesman*?"

"A very, very good one. He worked for a big brewery in the capital, selling a beer called... well, never mind what it was called. And Emery could sell beer to anyone. He would walk into a bar, start talking, and in ten minutes everybody in the bar was buying beer and listening to him. He was tall and thin, with a little beard and glasses, and he talked like music."

"So that's how he learned to give speeches," said Samuel slowly. "Selling beer."

"Exactly. He went to every bar in the city. And as he sold beer, he talked. About freedom. About dignity. About one great country, instead of hundreds of tribes fighting each other."

---

Emery founded a political party. It was the only party that wanted the whole country united, not divided by tribe. He was arrested for causing riots. He was put in prison. And while he was in prison, the European government realised it could not hold the country any more. It agreed to independence.

They let Emery out of prison, with the marks of the handcuffs still on his wrists, and flew him to the capital for the independence talks.

His party won the elections. At thirty-four years old, the beer salesman became the first prime minister of his country.

---

On the day of independence, there was a great ceremony. The European king came, in his white uniform. He gave a speech. And in that speech, he praised his great-granduncle, the old king who had owned the country like a farm, as a hero who had brought civilisation to Africa.

The Africans in the hall sat silent. Their grandparents' hands had been cut off by that "hero's" soldiers.

Emery was not supposed to speak. He was not on the programme.

He stood up anyway.

He walked to the microphone, in front of the king, the diplomats, the cameras, and the radio broadcasting to the whole country. And he said:

*"We have known the ironies, the insults, the blows that we had to endure morning, noon and night, because we were Black."*

He talked about the forced labour. The stolen land. The prisons. The humiliation. He said that independence had not been *given* to them. It had been *won*, through tears, fire and blood.

The king's face went white.

And across the whole country, in every village with a radio, people stood up and cheered.

---

"That was very brave," said Luca.

"It was very brave," said Nonno Beppe. "And it was the beginning of his death."

---

The new country was not ready. Within days, the army mutinied. The richest province, full of copper and diamonds, declared itself a separate country, supported by the old colonial power and by foreign mining companies. Emery asked the United Nations for help. They were slow. He asked other countries for help. The great powers of the world began to whisper that Emery was dangerous, that he was too independent, that he couldn't be controlled.

Only ten weeks after independence, he was overthrown. Soldiers put him under house arrest, surrounded by guards.

He escaped, hidden in a car, at night, in the rain, trying to reach his supporters in the east. But he stopped in villages along the way, and people recognised him, and crowds gathered, and he couldn't resist speaking to them. The soldiers caught up with him at a river crossing.

They beat him. They put him in prison. And in January, they put him on a plane and flew him to the breakaway province, to the men who hated him most.

They beat him on the plane. They beat him when he landed. And that same night, in the dark forest, by the light of car headlights, a firing squad, under the command of European officers, shot him and two of his companions against a tree.

He was thirty-five years old. He had been prime minister for less than three months.

---

Samuel was holding his knees.

"What did they do with his body?" he asked. "My nonna said..."

Nonno Beppe hesitated.

"Tell him, Nonno," said Luca quietly. "He wants to know."

"They wanted to make him disappear completely. So that there would be no grave, no place to bring flowers. A few days later, a European police officer dug up the bodies, cut them into pieces, and dissolved them in acid."

Nobody said anything.

"But the man who did it kept something. A tooth. For more than sixty years, it was hidden away in Europe. And just a few years ago, they finally gave it back. His children flew it home, in a small coffin, and the whole country came out into the streets to bury it. A tooth. That's all that came home of him."

Samuel's eyes were full of tears behind his glasses.

---

"But Samuel," said Nonno Beppe, leaning forward, "they could not dissolve his words. Before he died, from his prison, he wrote a letter to his wife. Your nonna knows it. Many people in your country know it by heart."

And Nonno Beppe recited, slowly:

*"Do not weep for me, my companion. I know that my country, which suffers so much, will know how to defend its independence and its liberty...*

*No brutality, no torture has ever forced me to ask for mercy, for I prefer to die with my head held high, my faith unshakeable, and with profound trust in the destiny of my country...*

*History will one day have its say. Africa will write its own history, and it will be a history of glory and dignity."*

---

Samuel wiped his eyes. Then, very slowly, he reached into his pocket and took out the little folded papers he had been writing all week.

"I guessed all of them," he said. "Except tonight's. Tonight's I didn't need to guess." He looked up. "Nonno Beppe, my great-grandfather was there. At independence. In the hall. My nonna says he heard the speech."

Nonno Beppe smiled.

"Then you are part of the story too."

Nonna Rosa came out onto the porch, with a plate of lasagna in one hand and a plate of mikate in the other. She had finally got the recipe. Nobody knew how.

She sat down between the boys. Garibaldi climbed onto Samuel's lap for the first time and fell asleep.

And the four of them sat there in the warm Italian night, an old man, an old woman, a boy whose family came from the coast near Rome and a boy whose family came from the heart of Africa, and talked until midnight about all the people who had died so that they could sit there, free.

> ### ✝ The Lesson of the Seventh Night
> **"And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell."** *(Matthew 10:28)*
>
> They beat him, shot him, and dissolved his body in acid so that nothing would be left. But they could not touch his soul, and they could not erase his words. "I prefer to die with my head held high, my faith unshakeable."
>
> Human power can take everything from you except what belongs to God. Truth, dignity, and the soul are out of their reach. And history, as Emery said, will one day have its say. Better still, God will have His say, and His judgement is always just.

## EPILOGUE: Questa È la Loro Storia
### *This Is Their Story*

Samuel unfolded his papers one by one. He had guessed them all. Did you?

---

**Chapter One — "Manu" was RANI LAKSHMIBAI, the Rani of Jhansi**, India, c. 1828–1858
Born Manikarnika ("Manu") in Varanasi, she grew up at the court of the Peshwa learning horsemanship and swordsmanship alongside the boys. She married Gangadhar Rao, the Maharaja of Jhansi. After his death, the British East India Company used the "Doctrine of Lapse" to refuse recognition of their adopted son, Damodar Rao, and annexed Jhansi. She is remembered for declaring *"Main apni Jhansi nahi doongi"* ("I will not give up my Jhansi"). A leader of the Indian Rebellion of 1857, she escaped the siege of Jhansi in 1858 with her son, and was killed in battle at Kotah-ki-Serai near Gwalior on 18 June 1858, dressed as a cavalry soldier. The British commander, Sir Hugh Rose, called her the most dangerous of the rebel leaders. She is one of India's greatest national heroes.

**Chapter Two — "François" was TOUSSAINT LOUVERTURE**, Haiti, c. 1743–1803
Born enslaved on the Bréda plantation in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, he was a sickly child nicknamed *Fatras-Bâton* ("sickly stick"). He became a skilled horseman, coachman and healer, was freed, and in 1791 joined the great slave uprising. He became its greatest general and the governor of the colony, and abolished slavery there. His name "Louverture" means "the opening." In 1802, Napoleon Bonaparte sent an army under his brother-in-law, General Leclerc. Toussaint was invited to a meeting, arrested, and deported to France, where he said: *"In overthrowing me, you have cut down in Saint-Domingue only the trunk of the tree of liberty. It will spring up again by the roots, for they are numerous and deep."* He died in a freezing cell at the Fort de Joux in the Jura mountains on 7 April 1803. Haiti declared independence on 1 January 1804, the first nation founded by a successful slave revolt.

**Chapter Three — "Pepe" was JOSÉ MARTÍ**, Cuba, 1853–1895
Poet, journalist and the founder of the Cuban Revolutionary Party. At 16, he was sentenced to six years of hard labour for a letter, and he wore a ring made from his shackle for the rest of his life. He spent most of his life in exile, including about fifteen years in New York, where he wrote for newspapers across Latin America and created the children's magazine *La Edad de Oro*. Lines from his *Versos Sencillos* became the lyrics of the famous song *"Guantanamera,"* and his poem *"Cultivo una rosa blanca"* speaks of growing a white rose even for the cruel. He landed in Cuba in April 1895 to join the War of Independence, and was killed at Dos Ríos on 19 May 1895, charging on a white horse in his first battle. He is Cuba's national hero, the "Apostle of Cuban Independence."

**Chapter Four — "Johanna" was HANNIE SCHAFT** *(Jannetje Johanna Schaft)*, the Netherlands, 1920–1945
A law student in Amsterdam from Haarlem, she refused to sign the loyalty declaration demanded by the Nazi occupiers and joined the armed Dutch resistance. She helped Jewish friends hide and carried out dangerous missions. Known to the Germans as "the girl with the red hair," she dyed her hair black. She was arrested at a checkpoint on 21 March 1945 and executed in the dunes at Overveen on 17 April 1945, less than three weeks before the Netherlands was liberated. When the first shot only wounded her, she is reported to have said: *"Ik schiet beter"* ("I shoot better"). Queen Wilhelmina attended her state reburial in November 1945. A statue of her stands in Haarlem.

**Chapter Five — "Abel" was AMÍLCAR CABRAL**, Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde, 1924–1973
Born in Bafatá to Cape Verdean parents, he studied agronomy in Lisbon. His agricultural census of Portuguese Guinea in the 1950s took him to villages all over the country and gave him a deep understanding of its people. He co-founded the PAIGC in 1956. After the Pidjiguiti massacre of striking dockworkers in 1959, the movement turned to armed struggle, and the war of independence began in 1963. His army built schools and clinics in liberated areas, and he is famous for the words *"Tell no lies... claim no easy victories."* He was assassinated in Conakry on 20 January 1973 by members of his own movement. Guinea-Bissau declared independence in September 1973, and his half-brother, Luís Cabral, became its first president.

**Chapter Six — "Michael" was MICHAEL COLLINS**, Ireland, 1890–1922
Born in County Cork, he worked as a post office clerk in London. He fought in the 1916 Easter Rising at the General Post Office in Dublin and was interned at Frongoch camp. During the Irish War of Independence, he was director of intelligence for the IRA, and was famously known for cycling openly around Dublin while the British offered a large reward for him. Known as "The Big Fellow," he was also famous for his rough wrestling and "a bit of ear." He helped negotiate the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921, calling it the "freedom to achieve freedom," and reportedly said he might have signed his "actual death warrant." He became commander-in-chief of the National Army during the Irish Civil War, and was killed in an ambush at Béal na Bláth, County Cork, on 22 August 1922, aged 31.

**Chapter Seven — "Emery" was PATRICE LUMUMBA**, Democratic Republic of the Congo, 1925–1961
Born in Onalua, he was educated at Protestant and Catholic mission schools, worked as a postal clerk, and became a successful beer salesman in Léopoldville. He founded the Mouvement National Congolais (MNC), the only major party committed to a united Congo, and was imprisoned before independence talks. He became the first Prime Minister of the independent Congo on 30 June 1960, the day he gave his famous unscheduled speech in front of King Baudouin of Belgium. Amid an army mutiny and the secession of Katanga, he was deposed in September 1960, captured, and flown to Katanga, where he was shot on 17 January 1961, aged 35, with his companions Maurice Mpolo and Joseph Okito, by a firing squad commanded by Belgian officers. His body was dissolved in acid. In 2022, Belgium returned a tooth, the only known remains, to his family. His last letter to his wife, Pauline, is one of the most famous documents in African history.

### Author's Note

The seven historical stories in this book follow the real lives and deaths of these seven people. Some details, conversations and moments are imagined, simplified, or drawn from popular tradition. Some famous scenes, such as the Rani's leap from the walls of Jhansi or Michael Collins' "bit of ear," come from legend and storytelling as much as from documents. The words given as quotations are those traditionally attributed to them. The quotation from Lumumba's speech and the passages from his final letter are shortened.

Nonno Beppe, Nonna Rosa, Luca, Samuel, his grandmother, and Garibaldi the cat are fictional.
